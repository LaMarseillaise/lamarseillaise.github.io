    From Latin to transformer attention

    Studying Latin gave me an intuitive starting point for understanding attention in machine learning. A translation emerges from relationships across the sentence: an adjective may belong to a distant noun; an ending helps identify a subject or object; a pronoun sends the reader back to an earlier referent. The nearest word is not always the most relevant one.

    A small class conflict

    A co-worker once wrote, “Missing SU due to class conflict.” They meant they would miss stand-up because they had a college lecture. I read “SU” as the Soviet Union—which, of course, had quite a lot to say about class conflict. Encountering some might understandably make a person miss it.

    The joke works because the same words support two coherent readings. A team’s meeting schedule favors one; political history makes the other available. Grammar alone does not settle the meaning: shared context matters too. That is another useful intuition for contextual representations in a transformer, though attention weights alone do not explain how a model resolves an ambiguity.

    A transformer also combines information across positions. In self-attention, each token’s representation produces a query, key, and value. Query–key comparisons produce scores; a softmax converts them into weights, which determine how value vectors are combined. Multiple heads and successive layers build contextual representations. Tokens may be word fragments, and positional information keeps word order available. A causal model restricts attention to earlier positions and the current one; an encoder can use context on both sides. See Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need, §3.2.

    The useful analogy is gathering context across distance. The lines in this notebook record explicit interpretations made by a reader. A model computes numerical attention weights using learned projections; those weights do not carry labels such as “subject,” “object,” or “same person.” A large weight is not a probability that two words share a referent, nor sufficient evidence of why the model gave an answer. Jain and Wallace’s Attention is not Explanation examines this interpretability problem.

    That distinction suggests a practical experiment: ask a model to propose grammatical links, then compare them with a reader’s annotations. Subject and object links are a dependency-parsing task; identifying expressions that refer to the same entity is a coreference task. Agreement can draw on morphology as well as syntax. These predictions should be checked separately rather than treated as a single measure of “understanding Latin.” The notebook currently records human annotations only.
