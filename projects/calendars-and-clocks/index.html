Read the same moment

One instant. Several faces.

Changing the way we write time does not change how much of it has passed. Pause the clocks, change the conventional timezone, and watch which readings stay fixed.

Interactive clock comparison: conventional 12/24-hour and decimal clocks use a selected civil timezone. The global hexadecimal clock and seasonal calendar use a fixed day boundary at 12:00 UTC.

Sixteen, all the way down.

A four-digit hexadecimal clock divides the day into 65,536 equal ticks. It counts from 0000 through FFFF, using A–F for the values ten through fifteen. Each tick lasts about 1.318 ordinary seconds.

In my version, 0000 is midnight at the geometric dateline, 180° longitude: 12:00 UTC. Everyone sees the same reading. There are no regional offsets or daylight saving adjustments.

The divisions are choices.

Our familiar clock divides a day into 24 hours, then sixty minutes and sixty seconds. A 12-hour display repeats its labels twice. Revolutionary France tried ten hours, each with one hundred minutes of one hundred seconds.

These displays divide a nominal 24-hour day differently. The browser model uses 86,400 ordinary seconds per day and does not display leap seconds. Visit the Revolutionary calendar and decimal clock.

The Sun does not consult a timetable

Local noon became a shared clock.

Earth turns through roughly 360° in a day: about 15° per hour. Local solar noon comes at different instants at different longitudes. A common civil clock makes coordination easier, but need not put the Sun highest at 12:00.

A zone is more than an offset.

A UTC offset describes one difference at one instant. A named zone such as America/Los_Angeles carries rules that can change with the date. Political borders and local choices bend the neat longitude picture; some offsets use half or quarter hours.

Crossing the date line changes the civil date label. It does not add a day to your life. For my global clock, the straight 180° meridian is a reference, independent of the political date line’s bends.

Daylight saving moves the labels.

A spring jump can leave an hour with no local time labels. An autumn change can use the same labels twice. An appointment saying only “01:30” on the repeated day is ambiguous without its offset or another way to identify the instant.

Not every place changes clocks, and those that do need not follow the same dates. The example below uses Los Angeles’s 2026 rules. It starts one minute before the transition; advance a minute to see the jump.

Repair the agreement

Eleven dates missing.
No days lost.

A year is not an integer number of days. An extra day every four years is a good approximation, but a small annual error becomes a large historical discrepancy.

What Gregorian reform solves

The Julian rule averages 365.25 days per year. It slowly lets the calendar drift against the seasons. The Gregorian reform sought to bring the March equinox back toward its ecclesiastical reference date and improve the calendar used to calculate Easter.

Its leap rule is: divisible by four, except centuries, unless divisible by four hundred. That gives 97 leap days per 400 years, averaging 365.2425 days. Thus 1900 is common, while 2000 is leap.

The reform corrected accumulated drift by changing date labels, then reduced future drift by changing the leap rule. It remains an approximation. U.S. Naval Observatory: calendars and leap rules.

Why October happened in November

The Bolshevik seizure of power began on October 25, 1917 in the Julian calendar then used in Russia: November 7 in the Gregorian calendar. “October Revolution” preserves the contemporary local label. It is one event described by two calendars, not a contradiction.

University of Warwick’s Modern Records Centre: the two dates

Always ask: whose calendar?

There was no universal night on which everyone changed. A date skipped in Britain might be a perfectly valid label elsewhere. The date tools on this page use the Gregorian rule throughout their supported range, including before local adoption; this extension is called proleptic Gregorian.

They do not reconstruct every jurisdiction’s historical civil calendar. Astronomers’ Julian Day numbers are yet another system: a continuous day count, not the Julian calendar.

Try another agreement

Four seasons.
Fifty-two complete weeks.

My seasonal calendar gives each season thirteen weeks of seven days. Four times 91 makes 364. The remaining day belongs to the year, outside every season and week.

Seasons I–IV each have 91 days. Year Day follows Season IV every year. In leap years, Leap Day falls between Seasons II and III. Both extra days have no weekday.

Extra days without broken weeks

Year Day closes the year near the December solstice. In leap years, Leap Day falls between Seasons II and III, near the June solstice. These are descriptive labels. Neither day advances the seasonal weekday count.

Each season therefore starts on Day 1. These are Days 1–7, not a claim that Monday or another Gregorian weekday stays aligned. Season I–IV avoids calling one hemisphere’s winter everybody’s winter.

The seasons have fixed lengths. They approximate the sky’s seasons, whose lengths differ. There is no annual astronomical recalculation. See the geometry of the seasons.

An epoch we can calculate from

The day containing the December 1999 solstice is the final intercalary day of 11999. With the day boundary at 0000, year 12000 begins on December 22, 1999 at 12:00 UTC, the next dateline midnight.

The abstract era places today’s year around 10,000 years beyond Common Era numbering: most of Gregorian 2026 falls in 12026. The seasonal year changes near December’s solstice, not January 1. We count from the modern anchor rather than claiming an observed ancient solstice.

The calendar explorer supports years 10001–19999, as an arithmetic extension only. It makes no claims about what people historically called those dates.

Four, one hundred, four hundred, four thousand.

Add a leap day when the displayed seasonal year is divisible by 4. Omit it for multiples of 100; restore it for multiples of 400; omit it for multiples of 4000.

Find a weekday in your head

Conway’s Doomsday Rule.

I sometimes use this in conversation to tell people the weekday on which they were born. The trick is to find one weekday shared by memorable dates in a year, then count from the nearest one.

In any Gregorian year, April 4, June 6, August 8, October 10 and December 12 share a weekday: the year’s doomsday. The intervals between them are whole numbers of weeks. That weekday moves as ordinary and leap years pass.

For November 23, 2026: the century anchor is Tuesday. The year calculation is 2 + 2 + 0 = 4; Tuesday plus four gives Saturday. November 7 is a doomsday. Sixteen days later is Monday.
Why divide the year by twelve?

Within a century, each block of twelve years contributes twelve ordinary weekday shifts and three leap-day shifts: fifteen in total, or just one after removing whole weeks. Count one for each block, add the leftover years, then add one for every four leftover years. The century anchor accounts for the century’s starting point.
