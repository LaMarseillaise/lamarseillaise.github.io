The ten-day décade

    A new measure of the world

    Time, remade.

    Revolutionary France sought a new beginning in the calendar itself: a civic era, a seasonal year, and a day divided by ten.

    Adopted in 1793, the Republican calendar counted Year I from 22 September 1792. Twelve months of thirty days were divided into three ten-day décades. Five complementary festival days completed the year, with a sixth in leap years. The original decree tied the new year to the autumn equinox at the Paris Observatory. Read the calendar decree of 4 Frimaire, Year II.

    The months evoked the landscape and agricultural year: Vendémiaire the vintage, Brumaire the mists, Frimaire the frost. The calendar continued through 1805; the Gregorian calendar returned on 1 January 1806. Decimal time had a shorter official life: its compulsory use was suspended on 7 April 1795. Surviving clocks sometimes display both systems on the same instrument. See a period clock in the Fondation Napoléon collection.

    The decimal ambition

    One day.
    A hundred thousand seconds.

    10hours / day
    100minutes / hour
    100seconds / minute

    A decimal hour lasts 2 hours and 24 ordinary minutes. A decimal minute lasts 86.4 ordinary seconds; a decimal second lasts 0.864 ordinary seconds. The day stays the same length; its subdivisions change.

    The clock and calendar shared the metric reform’s ambition to make measurement regular and systematic. The revolutionary metric system established decimal relationships among units of length, volume, and mass; its legacy developed into the modern International System of Units. Decimal time did not endure with it, and the modern SI second is not the revolutionary decimal second. NIST’s historical account of the SI traces that longer development.

    A note on interpretation

    An extrapolation, not an official modern calendar.

    The calendar’s brief historical use leaves its continuation open to interpretation. This implementation extends an arithmetic rule from my original code: leap years III, VII, XI, and every fourth year thereafter, omitting years ending in 99 unless the year is 399 modulo 400. It does not calculate the Paris autumn equinox, so its dates can differ from astronomical reconstructions.

    The epoch is 22 September 1792. The clock uses modern civil time in the selected location, including daylight-saving changes; it divides the displayed 24-hour clock reading into decimal units. It is neither historical Paris local solar time nor a measurement of elapsed time through a 23- or 25-hour daylight-saving day. The date follows that same location.
