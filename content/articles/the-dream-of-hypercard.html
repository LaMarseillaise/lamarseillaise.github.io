Around 1994, my father downloaded a game I remember as Land of the Fuzzies. My sister and I discovered that it was made in HyperCard, and that we could modify it. Before long, we were making games of our own, with buttons and fields triggering events and changes of scene.

I was about seven. The environment was simple and visual enough that I could take something I was playing with and begin changing how it worked. The buttons were already there. Their behavior was something I could alter. Making a game began with a game.

I distinctly remember daydreaming in my second-grade classroom about making things happen in something closer to real time. Perhaps I could move back and forth between two pages. Then I wondered whether a page could interact with itself in the same way. I think of that as an early intuition of recursion, although the memory is of an idea taking shape, rather than a precise algorithm. I was thinking about what I could make the computer do.

That experience has remained my reference point for developer tools. For many years, modern programming felt oddly archaic to me: enormous text editors, elaborate file structures, and command-line ceremony surrounding the thing I actually wanted to build. I had been able to make interactive software as a child. Why did making it now so often seem to require more distance from the result?

HyperCard's invitation to modify existing work was deliberate. In a Computer History Museum interview, Bill Atkinson described opening the buttons in someone else's stack, inspecting their scripts, and changing what they did. He wanted people with something they cared about to be able to express it in software. That description is very close to the experience I remember. Interview transcript, pp. 26–28.

I have never been especially interested in typing code for its own sake. I want to think about a problem and devise a solution. When there is a well-trodden path, I am happy to follow a convention and let the computer handle the repetitive work. HyperCard gave me an early experience of how direct that relationship could feel.

AI is making me reconsider what an authoring environment could be. I can describe a behavior and get an implementation to try. I can ask for a change without first becoming fluent in every detail of the machinery beneath it. There is something familiar about that feeling: an idea becomes a thing I can interact with, and interacting with it suggests the next change.

I am interested in bringing that conversation into a visual environment. Select a button. Describe what it should do. Try it in the scene where it belongs. The little sequence is appealing because the object, the intention, and the result remain close together. It is a direction to explore, rather than a product I have already built.

The childhood story also suggests a useful place to begin: something small enough to change. We began by modifying a game. An environment that makes those first changes approachable could give someone room to discover a larger ambition while they are already making things.

That is why I keep returning to HyperCard. It gave a seven-year-old a way to work on the ideas occupying his mind in class. I would like to explore what could give people that same freedom now.
