In a conversation about cosmic horror, I found myself attracted to the very thing that is often supposed to make the situation frightening: the possibility that there is something extraordinary to learn.

My imagined response to an encounter with the incomprehensible was fairly simple: “Oh, isn’t that interesting!”

The conversation eventually arrived at a more precise question. If I could be shown everything, would that be enough? I would prefer understanding, although being shown everything sounded appealing too. Given a request, I wanted first to become capable of accommodating the knowledge and understanding, and then to receive it.

What would I be asking for?

There is a difference between having information available and being able to do something with it. I can look at an unfamiliar equation and possess an exact copy of its symbols. Understanding asks more of me: how its parts relate, which assumptions it uses, what follows from it, and where it might cease to help.

An imagined library containing every answer makes the distinction vivid. If I cannot formulate the question, find the relevant answer, or connect it with anything I already know, possession of the library leaves much of the original difficulty intact.

So my request would include a change in the recipient. I would want the capacity to follow the explanation, not merely access to a larger collection of statements. That makes education part of the imagined gift.

The next question

There is also something appealing about an explanation that makes a new question possible. I understand one relationship, notice another uncertainty, and now have a more useful way to investigate it. Understanding can change what I am able to ask.

This is one reason I like small demonstrations. Two remembered quantities can suggest the circumference of Earth. A shadow and a distance can become a geometric argument. An enormous result can have a route into it that is small enough to follow.

For a tool intended to teach, I want that route to remain visible. An answer is useful; being able to retrace the steps, change an assumption, and see why the answer changes gives the learner something more portable.

A question I would leave open

The imagined request raises a further problem. If becoming capable of understanding something required a profound change in my mind, what would make the resulting person continuous with me?

I do not have to settle that question to find it interesting. It also cautions against treating “show me everything” as a simple request. We would need to ask what counts as receiving it, what counts as understanding it, and who is doing the understanding at the end.

For now, I can pursue a smaller version of the wish: find an idea that I cannot yet follow, and build a way to follow it. The pleasure is partly in what I learn and partly in becoming able to learn the next thing.
